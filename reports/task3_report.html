2. Model Choice Justification

LightGBM (gradient-boosted decision trees) was chosen over neural networks and linear models for four reasons:
  • Mixed data types: The feature set combines numeric values (ratings, duration), categorical variables (genre, country), and high-cardinality channel identifiers. LightGBM handles all of these natively with its histogram-based algorithm; no manual one-hot encoding, no embedding layers to tune.
  • Native missing-value handling: Channel 9 (Sport) is systematically missing f_7 through f_11 (100% null). LightGBM learns optimal splits for missing vs non-missing values automatically, treating null as a signal rather than a problem to impute away.
  • Training cost: ~2-5 seconds on CPU for 500 trees with early stopping. Daily retraining (Task 4 requirement) is trivially cheap. A comparable neural architecture would need GPU, minutes per training run, and hyperparameter tuning: 50-100× higher cost.
  • Explainability: Tree-based models produce interpretable feature importance by construction. SHAP values provide per-prediction explanations: for any timeslot, you can see exactly which features drove the forecast up or down and by how much. Linear models are explainable but can't capture non-linear interactions (genre × prime time, competitor strength × day of week); deep learning can capture interactions but resists explanation. LightGBM sits at the Pareto frontier of accuracy and explainability.

6. Strengths and Weaknesses

Where the model performs well

  • Stable, recurring programming: Regular timeslots with known genres and consistent scheduling; the model learns these patterns reliably.
  • Prime time: The 19:00–23:00 window has the most training data and the strongest feature signals (hour, is_prime_time interaction).
  • Channels with complete metadata: Channels 3, 4, and 54 benefit from genre and country features that help disambiguate similar timeslots.

Where the model falls short

  • Channel 9 (Sport): Missing f_7 through f_11 means the model relies almost entirely on temporal features and the 3mo prior. Live sports events create spikes that the model cannot anticipate without event metadata.
  • One-off events: Special broadcasts, premieres, live events; these deviate from the historical patterns the model has learned.
  • Tail shares: Very high or very low share values are harder to predict; these are rare events with less training signal.
  • Holidays: Seasonal anomalies where viewing behavior shifts (Christmas, New Year's Eve) appear in the data but the model has only 1–2 examples per year to learn from.

7. Future Improvements

  • External data: TV program guide data (program names, episode numbers, live/sport flag), holiday calendar, and weather data (bad weather → more TV viewers).
  • Lag features: Share at the same hour on the previous same-day-of-week; captures week-over-week momentum without leaking future data.
  • Target encoding: For genre × channel combinations, mean target encoding can capture non-linear interactions that raw categorical encoding misses.
  • Quantile regression: LightGBM supports quantile loss natively; predicting a prediction interval (e.g., 10th/90th percentile) gives uncertainty estimates useful for scheduling decisions.
  • Competitor data outside Nova group: The assignment provides only Nova channels, but real audience share is a zero-sum game across ALL channels. Including programming data from competing networks would improve accuracy.
  • Online learning: Instead of full daily retraining, incremental model updates (via LightGBM's refit or warm-start) reduce compute and allow the model to adapt to slow concept drift.
